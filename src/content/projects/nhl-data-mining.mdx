## Overview

This was my CMPT 496 final project at MacEwan University, which I did with my classmate
[Mark Said](https://github.com/saidm4) under the supervision of
[Dr. Mohamad El-Hajj](https://www.macewan.ca/academics/academic-departments/computer-science/our-people/profile/?profileid=elhajjm).
We studied the moments when one team has an extra skater and asked two questions:

- What makes a power play or penalty kill succeed?
- When should a team that's down by a goal pull its goalie to force overtime?

We wrote up the results in a [research paper (PDF)](/papers/nhl-data-mining.pdf).

## The data

We used Kaggle's [NHL Game Data](https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/martinellis/nhl-game-data), a
relational database of games, shifts, plays, penalties and player stats, plus season-by-season
player positions from [MoneyPuck](https://moneypuck.com/data.htm). We kept the seasons from 2010
on, when shift data begins, and focused on 5-on-4 power plays and 6-on-5 goalie pulls by teams
trailing by one.

The data doesn't say who was on a power play unit, and it doesn't record goalie pulls at all, so I
rebuilt both from 11.9 million shift records:

- **Power plays and penalty kills:** I matched every player's shifts against two-minute minor
  penalties to find who was on the ice for each 5-on-4, and used the skater count to tell the power
  play from the penalty kill. Accounting for overlapping and coincidental penalties left a clean set
  of 42,581 lineups.
- **Goalie pulls:** I traced each goalie's third-period shifts to find when they left the ice, then
  filtered out the exits that weren't desperation pulls: the end of the period, a goalie being
  replaced, and pulls for a delayed penalty. That left 1,788 pulls, each with the skaters on the ice
  and whether the game went to overtime.

## Rating players by situation

No single stat says how well someone plays on the power play or penalty kill. Plus-minus comes
closest, but it measures the team more than the player: everyone on the ice gets credit for a goal,
whether they touched the puck or not. So we built an effectiveness rating, adapted from Dom
Luszczyszyn's [Game Score](https://hockey-graphs.com/2016/07/13/measuring-single-game-productivity-an-introduction-to-game-score/):

- Each position has its own formula. Centres get credit for faceoff win percentage, and defencemen
  leave out plus-minus, which swamped their smaller goal and assist totals.
- On the power play the formulas reward offence (goals, assists, shots) and subtract giveaways. On
  the penalty kill, blocks, takeaways and hits take the place of giveaways.
- The weighted stats are divided by the player's ice time in that situation, and only count games
  up to the one being rated, so a rating never uses results from the future.
- Ratings are scaled from 0 to 1 and split into five equal-width levels, Lowest to Highest, to keep
  the models easy to explain.

## What we found

- **Size matters on special teams.** Clustering penalty kill units with k-means showed heavier
  groups earning higher effectiveness ratings, and a random forest predicting power play goals
  ranked player weight as the most important feature by a wide margin, ahead of height. Position,
  handedness and nationality mattered much less.
- **Pull the goalie with 2:35 to 1:42 left.** A decision tree trained on the goalie pulls split
  first on pull time alone: pulls with under 58 seconds left were predicted to end in regulation,
  with too little time to score. Its later splits point to 2:35 to 1:42 remaining as the window
  most likely to force overtime.
